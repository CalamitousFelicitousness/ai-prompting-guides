# Gemini Omni: prompting and usage guide

<rules id="global">

- This guide covers prompt craft only. For endpoints, parameters, resolution and duration limits, input counts, and code, consult the specific provider or proxy's API docs; they differ and are out of scope here.
- Gemini Omni is a different prompt scheme from Gemini Image (Nano Banana). Do not carry the image rules across. Two of them invert here: plain negatives work, and references are bound with tags rather than positional phrases.
- Video is the output. Text, images, and video are inputs; the model produces video with audio.
- The model cuts by default. Left alone it builds a short multi-shot narrative, so a single unbroken take has to be requested.
- Audio is generated with the picture. Describe the track you want, or you get the model's guess.
- Generation and editing want opposite prompt lengths. Be detailed when generating; be brief when editing.
- English is the supported prompt language. Other languages are not evaluated and may vary.

</rules>

## Model names and aliases

Google ships every generative-media model under two names, and the nickname carries less information than the official name. Prefer the Gemini name and translate the nickname on sight. Omni has no nickname; the rest of the table is here because the image models are the ones most often confused with it.

| Gemini name (preferred) | Gemini API model ID | Nickname | Line | Role |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Gemini Omni Flash | `gemini-omni-flash-preview` | none | Omni | Video output with native audio; this guide |
| Gemini 3 Pro Image | `gemini-3-pro-image` | Nano Banana Pro | Pro | Top tier: highest world knowledge, brand consistency, precision control |
| Gemini 3.1 Flash Image | `gemini-3.1-flash-image` | Nano Banana 2 | Flash | Generalist default; best multi-reference handling and consistency |
| Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite Image | `gemini-3.1-flash-lite-image` | Nano Banana 2 Lite | Flash Lite | Speed and scale; not built for many references or long edit chains |
| Gemini 2.5 Flash Image | `gemini-2.5-flash-image` | Nano Banana | Flash (legacy) | Superseded; Google recommends moving to Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite Image |

<rules id="naming">

- Translate the nickname to the Gemini name before deciding anything. The Gemini name states the generation and the tier separately; the nickname collapses them and drops one.
- "Nano Banana 2" is not the successor to "Nano Banana Pro". The 2 counts generations on the Flash line, so Nano Banana 2 is a newer generation than Nano Banana Pro while sitting below it in tier. Pro is still the strongest model.
- Cloud and Vertex surfaces append `-preview` to the same models (`gemini-3-pro-image-preview`, `gemini-3.1-flash-image-preview`). Same weights, different surface.
- Hosts invent tier labels that do not exist upstream. Names like Ultra or Multi on a hosted variant describe that host's packaging, not a Google model, and they change nothing about how the prompt is written.
- The same model often appears twice on one host, once under its nickname and once under its Gemini ID. Same weights.
- The image models and Gemini Omni are different prompt schemes with separate guides. Check which one you are in before applying a rule from memory.
- Imagen and Veo are separate Google models on separate prompt schemes, and neither belongs to this family.

</rules>

## TL;DR

## Models and modes

`gemini-omni-flash-preview` is the first release of the Omni line and currently the only one. One model serves every workflow; what changes is which inputs you supply.

- Text to video: prompt only.
- Image to video: supply an image and describe the motion. The model decides how to use the image unless you tell it, so say whether the image is the opening frame or only a guide.
- Reference to video: supply one or more images as subjects, styles, or objects, and bind each to a role.
- Edit: supply a video (or continue from one the model just made) and describe the change.

Hosts usually expose a way to declare which of these you intend. When it is not declared the model infers the mode from the prompt, so write the prompt so the intent is unambiguous.

## How the model reads prompts

- It is a multimodal reasoning model applied to video, so it responds to intent and world knowledge, not keywords. Naming a genre, a technique, or a real subject buys you its conventions.
- Generation rewards specificity. Vague motion prompts underperform: "make it move" produces far less than a described camera move, subject motion, and environmental effect.
- Editing rewards restraint. Over-describing an edit causes changes you did not ask for; the owner's own guidance shows the verbose version as the mistake and the one-line version as the fix.
- It narrates. Given room, it will invent a small story and cut between shots to tell it. That is useful for pitch and sizzle work, and it is the thing to suppress for a single clean take.
- It syncs. Text, effects, and audio can be tied to actions in the frame or to beats in the music, which is the capability that most separates it from a plain video generator.
- It is conversational and stateful. Each edit turn builds on the last while holding what you did not mention, so refine across turns instead of rewriting one long prompt.
- Plain negatives work here. "No dialogue" and "Do not show the drawing" are documented, effective instructions, not something to rephrase positively.

## Prompt structure

<rules id="structure">

- Cover subject and action, camera movement, lighting and mood, then sound design. Add a shot-continuity instruction whenever the number of cuts matters.
- Name motion explicitly. Say what the subject does, what the camera does, and what the environment does; these are three separate things and the model uses all three.
- Put the sound design in its own clause at the end. A short "Sound design: ..." tail keeps it from being read as part of the visual description.
- Close with any plain negatives. Short, blunt exclusions belong at the end where they read as constraints rather than content.
- Owner examples append the aspect ratio and clip length as a short tail ("Cinematic, 16:9.", "10s."). The reliable control for both is host-side, but stating them costs nothing.

</rules>

<template id="general">

{shot-continuity instruction} of {subject with concrete attributes} {action}, {environment}. {Camera movement.} {Subject motion and environmental detail.} {Lighting and mood.} Sound design: {ambience, effects, music}. {Plain negatives.}

</template>

<example use_case="directed-single-shot">

```text
Continuous, unbroken handheld shot of a fluffy tabby cat sitting on a sunny windowsill, looking out into a leafy garden. The cat's tail twitches slowly, and its ears rotate slightly toward ambient noises. Sunbeams illuminate dust motes in the air. Sound design: Gentle breeze, distant bird chirps. No dialogue.
```

*Why: the continuity instruction comes first so the model does not cut, subject motion and environmental motion are named separately, and the sound design and the negative sit in their own closing clauses*

</example>

## Shot control

<rules id="shots">

- To get one take, say so: "in a single unbroken scene", "in a single continuous shot", or "no scene cuts". Any of the three works and none is a special syntax.
- To get several shots, you can simply let the default run, or take control of the cuts with timing instructions.
- Cutting rhythm is directable in plain words: "every 2s cut to a new frame", or "in a rapid fire sequence, change the scene to a new location every half a second".
- When you want a specific number of beats, describe them rather than counting shots. The model blocks the coverage from the described action.

</rules>

<example use_case="rapid-fire-sequence">

```text
A rapid fire sequence showing one unusual object per letter of the alphabet, each sitting alone on a plain table, roughly nine frames per item. Each object gets a lower third that looks like black marker written on a slip of paper in the bottom left, showing only its letter. One object and one lower third on screen at a time. The last frame is a slip of paper reading "THE END". Calm smooth music throughout.
```

*Why: takes control of the default cutting instead of suppressing it, fixes the pacing, the on-screen label style, and the one-at-a-time constraint, and pins a specific final frame*

</example>

## Timing and event synchronization

Two different clocks are available. Use wall-clock timing to choreograph, and event triggers to react to something already in the footage.

<rules id="timing">

- Wall-clock timing takes natural language and needs no precise syntax: "after 3 seconds, a woman enters the scene", "at 5s the chorus starts in the background audio".
- A bracketed timecode list is also accepted for laying out a whole shot as a timeline.
- Event triggers bind a change to a moment in the supplied video rather than to a time: "When the person touches the mirror, ...", "When the hand opens, ...". This is the most precise way to place an effect in footage you did not shoot to a click track.
- Audio can be the trigger or the target. Sounds can be synchronized to an action, and visual events can be synchronized to the music.
- One trigger per prompt. Chaining several conditional events in a single instruction is where this technique starts to fail.

</rules>

<template id="timecode">

```text
[0-3s] {what happens first}
[3-6s] {what changes}
[6-10s] {where it resolves}
```

</template>

<example use_case="timecode-timeline">

```text
[0-3s] A person is walking down an empty corridor, camera tracking behind them
[3-6s] They stop and turn around, overhead lights flickering once
[6-10s] They start running back toward the camera as the lights cut out behind them
```

*Why: the bracketed timeline places three beats on an explicit clock, each naming both the subject action and a camera or lighting change so the shot has somewhere to go*

</example>

<example use_case="event-triggered-edit">

```text
When the person touches the mirror, make the mirror ripple beautifully like liquid, and the person's arm turns into reflective mirror material. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: the change is bound to an action already in the source footage rather than to a timestamp, so the effect lands on the frame where it belongs no matter how the take was shot*

</example>

<example use_case="audio-synchronized-effect">

```text
Add harp sounds synchronized to when I touch each fern leaf. Change the leaves to semi-translucent bioluminescent plant life with fireflies drifting around them that react in time with the sounds, subtle bokeh depth of field, light reflecting onto the walls of the room. Keep the room structure the same.
```

*Why: sound is generated on a visual cue and the visual reaction is then locked to that sound, using the same trigger in both directions, with the room pinned so only the named elements change*

</example>

## Audio

<rules id="audio">

- A soundtrack is generated whether or not you ask, so describe the one you want when it matters. Music especially will otherwise be the model's choice.
- Say what kind of music and where it sits: "include calm background music", "a high energy techno beat", "a low tinny radio broadcast in the background, playing a song".
- Ask for a voiceover explicitly when you want narration. It is not implied by the presence of a subject.
- Suppress what you do not want with plain negatives: "No dialogue", "No extra sound effects", "No music, just realistic real world sound".
- Watch the tail of the clip. "No voice cuts at the end" is a documented fix for narration that runs out of room.

</rules>

<example use_case="narrated-explainer">

```text
A claymation explainer of protein folding, everything made out of clay, stop motion, with a compelling voiceover explaining each stage accurately. No hands in frame. No music, just the narration and subtle stop-motion foley. Do not add text.
```

*Why: names the medium and the technique so the model supplies their conventions, calls for the voiceover explicitly rather than assuming it, and uses three plain negatives to strip the things this format usually attracts*

</example>

## Text in video

<rules id="text">

- Quote the words you want rendered. The model places and animates text legibly, including per-word timing and animation.
- Name incidental background text too. Signs, storefronts, and number plates will otherwise be invented; specifying them is how you control them.
- Text can be bound to timing and to actions, which is how you get word-by-word reveals and lower thirds that change on cue.
- Suppress unwanted lettering with a plain "Do not add text."

</rules>

<example use_case="word-by-word-text">

```text
One word on the screen at a time: "did, you, know, that, Omni, can, do, awesome, text?" Each word appears for one second in a different animated style, paced to a rhythm. Sizzle reel energy, upbeat music. No dialogue.
```

*Why: the full string is quoted, the per-word timing and the style variation are stated as rules rather than described per word, and the negative stops the words being spoken as well as shown*

</example>

## Reference images and role tags

Media is bound to a job with angle-bracket tags. Simple tags are the recommended form; the explicit declaration block is for cases with several images in several roles.

<rules id="references">

- `<FIRST_FRAME>` marks an image as the opening frame of the video.
- `<IMAGE_REF_N>` marks an image as a reference, numbered from zero in upload order. Place the tag next to the thing it supplies, inline in the sentence.
- One role per input. Style, subject, and object references are different jobs; give each image exactly one and put the tag where that role is mentioned.
- For several images in several roles, prefix the prompt with a declaration block and append a plain-language guiding instruction at the end saying how each image should be used.
- When the input is a guide rather than content, say so and say what must not appear: "using the drawing only as a guide for movement, do not show the drawing in the final video".
- Do not reference more than one video. Reasoning across multiple videos is unsupported and degrades the result.

</rules>

<template id="simple-tags">

```text
in the style of <IMAGE_REF_0> a woman <IMAGE_REF_1> is walking
```

</template>

<template id="explicit-declaration">

```text
[# Sources <FIRST_FRAME>@Image1] [# References <IMAGE_REF_0>@Image2] {prompt text referring to <IMAGE_REF_0>}. Use Image1 as the starting frame. Use Image2 as a reference for the video generation.
```

</template>

<example use_case="multi-reference-sequence">

```text
[0-3s] A studio fashion sequence. Starting with woman <IMAGE_REF_0>, she is holding <IMAGE_REF_1>
[3-6s] Then we see the man <IMAGE_REF_2> holding <IMAGE_REF_3>
[6-10s] And finally another woman <IMAGE_REF_4> who is holding <IMAGE_REF_5> while walking.
```

*Why: six references each welded to one role at the point they appear, laid out on a timeline so each pair gets its own segment rather than competing inside one shot*

</example>

<example use_case="cross-modal-transfer">

```text
Apply the pose and motion from the input video to the character from <IMAGE_REF_0>, and apply the style from <IMAGE_REF_1> to the new video. Keep the character's proportions and costume as they are in the reference.
```

*Why: one role per input across three sources (motion from the video, subject from the first image, style from the second), with the invariants named so the borrowed motion does not drag the character's design with it*

</example>

## By mode

### Text to video

<example use_case="text-to-video">

```text
A marble rolling fast on a chain reaction style track, continuous smooth shot. Close following camera, shallow depth of field, warm workshop lighting. Sound design: the marble ticking over wooden rails, springs releasing, a distant clock. No music.
```

*Why: a short concrete subject and a continuity instruction do most of the work, and the sound-design tail gives the audio track something specific to build from*

</example>

### Image to video

<rules id="i2v">

- Say what role the image plays. The model decides for itself otherwise, and a supplied drawing may end up rendered in frame rather than animated.
- Describe camera movement, subject motion, and environmental effects. These are what the still cannot supply.
- Use a high-resolution source; vague prompts on a soft image produce the weakest results in this mode.

</rules>

<example use_case="image-to-video">

```text
Turn this into realistic footage, using the drawing only as a guide for movement, do not show the drawing in the final video. The fish breaks the surface, water sheeting off its fins, spray catching the light before it falls back. Slow motion, shallow depth of field. Sound design: the splash and a low underwater rumble.
```

*Why: states the image's role and forbids the obvious failure (rendering the drawing itself), then supplies the motion, the physical detail, and the audio that the still cannot carry*

</example>

### Reference to video

<example use_case="reference-to-video">

```text
When the hand opens, reveal a photorealistic flying machine based on <IMAGE_REF_0>, floating above the palm with its propeller spinning, casting a soft shadow onto the skin. No music, just realistic real world sound. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: combines an event trigger with a reference-bound object, names how the object interacts with the existing frame (shadow on the skin), and strips the score so the effect reads as physical*

</example>

## Conversational editing

Editing is stateful across turns. Each instruction builds on the last while the model preserves what you did not mention, so make one change per turn.

<rules id="edit">

- Lead with an imperative verb. It can name the change (Add, Replace, Redraw, Restore) or the constraint (Keep), but the opening clause must instruct rather than describe. A declarative setup such as "Image 1 provides the foundation, Image 2 provides the face" states the plan instead of issuing it and pushes the real instruction back by a sentence or two.
- Keep it short. A single sentence naming one change outperforms a paragraph describing the whole result.
- Add "Keep everything else the same" whenever you are changing one aspect. It is the standard invariant phrase in this scheme.
- Name the outcome, not the procedure. "Make the phone invisible" beats describing removal and background reconstruction.
- Make one change per turn and iterate. Stacking edits into one instruction is what causes drift.
- Camera, style, lighting, and on-screen text are all editable in plain words on an existing clip.

</rules>

<example use_case="simple-edit">

```text
Add a cat that jumps onto his lap, he begins to pet it. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: the short form of an edit the owner shows failing when written out in full; naming the outcome and pinning the rest is what keeps the change scoped*

</example>

<example use_case="removal-edit">

```text
Make the phone invisible. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: states the desired end state in three words rather than describing removal and background fill, which is the phrasing that produces clean removals here*

</example>

<example use_case="restyle-edit">

```text
Change the camera angle to be over the violinist's shoulder. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: camera position is an editable property of an existing clip in this scheme, and the invariant phrase stops the re-framing from re-rendering the performer or the room*

</example>

## Negative prompts and exclusions

<rules id="negatives">

- There is no negative field, and none is needed. Write plain negatives into the prompt: "No dialogue", "No embellishments", "No extra sound effects", "Don't add text", "no wires", "no hands".
- This is the opposite of the Gemini Image rule. Do not rewrite these into positive descriptions.
- Put them at the end, in short blunt clauses, so they read as constraints rather than scene content.
- Pair a negative with the positive it protects when the distinction matters: "No music, just realistic real world sound".

</rules>

## Requests the model will not fulfil

<rules id="unsupported">

- Do not prompt for video extension or for interpolation between a first and last frame. Neither is supported.
- Do not reference or compare more than one video in a prompt. Multi-video reasoning degrades the output.
- Do not ask for voice editing, and do not plan on supplying an audio reference; audio comes from description only.
- Do not expect a system-instruction layer or a separate negative field to shape behavior. Everything goes in the prompt.
- Regional restrictions apply to editing uploaded video and to uploaded images of certain people; these vary by host and are out of scope here.

</rules>

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns

<rules id="avoid">

- Unwanted cuts: the default is a multi-shot narrative. Ask for a single continuous shot when you want one take.
- Over-describing an edit: long editing instructions cause unintended changes. Name one outcome and add "Keep everything else the same".
- Rewriting negatives as positives: a habit carried from the Gemini Image scheme. Plain negatives are the documented advice here.
- Untagged references: with several images, leaving roles to inference blends them. Bind each with its own tag at the point it is mentioned.
- Letting a guide image render: state when an input is only a movement or style guide and forbid it appearing in frame.
- Silent audio assumptions: a soundtrack is generated regardless. Describe it or suppress it.
- Unspecified background text: signs and labels get invented. Name them if they matter.
- Chained event triggers: one "when X happens" per prompt. Several conditions in one instruction is where this breaks down.
- Non-English prompts: only English is evaluated, so results in other languages are unpredictable.
- Vague motion in image-to-video: "make it move" wastes the mode. Describe camera, subject, and environment motion separately.

</rules>
