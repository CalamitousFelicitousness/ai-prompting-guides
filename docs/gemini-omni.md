# Gemini Omni: prompting and usage guide

<rules id="global">

- This guide covers prompt craft only. For endpoints, parameters, resolution and duration limits, input counts, and code, consult the specific provider or proxy's API docs; they differ and are out of scope here.
- Gemini Omni is a different prompt scheme from Gemini Image (Nano Banana). Do not carry the image rules across. Two of them invert here: plain negatives work, and edit instructions stay short.
- Video is the output. Text, images, and video are inputs; the model produces video with audio.
- The model cuts by default. Left alone it builds a short multi-shot narrative, so a single unbroken take has to be requested.
- Audio is generated with the picture, dialogue included. Describe the track and the voices you want, or you get the model's guess.
- Describe what each input is for in plain words. The owner has deprecated the angle-bracket control tags and says to describe how each image or video should be used in natural language instead.
- Generation and editing want opposite prompt lengths. Be detailed when generating; be brief when editing.
- English is the supported prompt language. Other languages are not evaluated and may vary.

</rules>

## Model names and aliases

Google ships every generative-media model under two names, and the nickname carries less information than the official name. Prefer the Gemini name and translate the nickname on sight. Omni has no nickname; the rest of the table is here because the image models are the ones most often confused with it.

<rules id="naming">

- Translate the nickname to the Gemini name before deciding anything. The Gemini name states the generation and the tier separately; the nickname collapses them and drops one.
- "Nano Banana 2" is not the successor to "Nano Banana Pro". The 2 counts generations on the Flash line, so Nano Banana 2 is a newer generation than Nano Banana Pro while sitting below it in tier. Pro is still the strongest model.
- Cloud and Vertex surfaces append `-preview` to the same models (`gemini-3-pro-image-preview`, `gemini-3.1-flash-image-preview`). Same weights, different surface.
- Hosts invent tier labels that do not exist upstream. Names like Ultra or Multi on a hosted variant describe that host's packaging, not a Google model, and they change nothing about how the prompt is written.
- The same model often appears twice on one host, once under its nickname and once under its Gemini ID. Same weights.
- The image models and Gemini Omni are different prompt schemes with separate guides. Check which one you are in before applying a rule from memory.
- Imagen and Veo are separate Google models on separate prompt schemes, and neither belongs to this family.

</rules>

## Models and modes

`gemini-omni-1.2-flash` is the current model. `gemini-omni-1.1-flash` is the previous release, and the owner is routing its requests to 1.2; the original preview model is shut down. Prompts written for either earlier model carry over, apart from the role tags covered under references below.

The owner's case for 1.2 over 1.1: it follows image, video and audio references more closely, holds faces, garments, packaging type and brand colors steadier across camera moves, makes fewer unwanted jump cuts in continuous camera work, and gives more natural dialogue, clearer lip sync and more distinct voices when several people speak. Its published comparisons show the largest gains in consistency and in identity across several references, and the smallest in prompt adherence.

One model serves every workflow; what changes is which inputs you supply.

- Text to video: prompt only.
- Image to video: supply an image and describe the motion. The model decides how to use the image unless you tell it, so say whether the image is the opening frame or only a guide.
- First and last frame: supply two images and describe the transition between them.
- Reference to video: supply images (and, when generating, short reference clips) of subjects, places, styles or motion, and say what each one is for.
- Edit: supply a video, or continue from one the model just made, and describe the change.
- Extend: supply a video, or continue from one the model just made, and describe what happens next.

The model infers the mode from the prompt and the inputs. Some hosts expose a mode switch; the owner says to try plain prompting first and use the switch only as a fallback, because forcing the mode adds constraints. Write the prompt so the intent is unambiguous.

## How the model reads prompts

- It is a multimodal reasoning model applied to video, so it responds to intent and world knowledge, not keywords. Naming a genre, a technique, or a real subject buys you its conventions. The owner contrasts it with Veo here: Veo needs precise instructions, while Omni can be told what you want and left to supply the detail.
- Generation rewards specificity. Vague motion prompts underperform: "make it move" produces far less than a described camera move, subject motion, and environmental effect.
- Editing rewards restraint. Over-describing an edit causes changes you did not ask for; the owner's own guidance shows the verbose version as the mistake and the one-line version as the fix.
- It narrates. Given room, it will invent a small story and cut between shots to tell it. That is useful for pitch and sizzle work, and it is the thing to suppress for a single clean take.
- It syncs. Text, effects, and audio can be tied to actions in the frame or to beats in the music, which is the capability that most separates it from a plain video generator.
- It is conversational and stateful. Each edit or extension turn builds on the last while holding what you did not mention, so refine across turns instead of rewriting one long prompt.
- Plain negatives work here. "No dialogue" and "Do not show the drawing" are documented, effective instructions, not something to rephrase positively.

## Prompt structure

<rules id="structure">

- Cover subject and action, camera framing and movement, style, lighting and location, then sound design. The owner's prompt guide names the same elements: shot framing and motion, style, lighting, location, action.
- Name motion explicitly. Say what the subject does, what the camera does, and what the environment does; these are three separate things and the model uses all three.
- Write it as direction to a crew rather than a description of a finished frame. Film terms (dolly zoom, whip pan, snap zoom, orbit) are understood without explanation.
- Put the sound design in its own clause at the end. A short "Sound design: ..." tail keeps it from being read as part of the visual description.
- Hold a look for the whole clip with a persistence clause: "Maintain the microscope lens effect throughout the entire video."
- Close with any plain negatives. Short, blunt exclusions belong at the end where they read as constraints rather than content.
- Owner examples append the aspect ratio and clip length as a short tail ("Cinematic, 16:9.", "10s."). The model reads a length in the prompt, but a length set on the request overrides it, so the reliable control is host-side.

</rules>

<template id="general">

{shot-continuity instruction} of {subject with concrete attributes} {action}, {environment}. {Camera movement.} {Subject motion and environmental detail.} {Lighting and mood.} Sound design: {ambience, effects, music}. {Plain negatives.}

</template>

<example use_case="directed-single-shot">

```text
Continuous, unbroken handheld shot of a fluffy tabby cat sitting on a sunny windowsill, looking out into a leafy garden. The cat's tail twitches slowly, and its ears rotate slightly toward ambient noises. Sunbeams illuminate dust motes in the air. Sound design: Gentle breeze, distant bird chirps. No dialogue.
```

*Why: the continuity instruction comes first so the model does not cut, subject motion and environmental motion are named separately, and the sound design and the negative sit in their own closing clauses*

</example>

<example use_case="persistent-look">

```text
A microscopic view of iridescent marine diatoms, displaying intricate, glass-like silica shells with breathtaking natural symmetry. The colors range from deep volcanic amber and warm copper to vibrant turquoise and violet, mimicking the rich palette of earth and ocean. Tiny, delicate structures glow softly against a clean dark field background. High-fidelity scientific imaging, sharp details, organic textures, micro-photography. Maintain the microscope lens effect throughout the entire video.
```

*Why: the owner's resolution demo. The palette and the imaging style are named concretely, and the closing sentence holds the optical treatment for the whole clip instead of letting it drift once the model starts cutting*

</example>

## Shot control

<rules id="shots">

- To get one take, say so: "in a single unbroken scene", "in a single continuous shot", "one continuous shot, no jump cuts", "no scene cuts", or the film term "oner". None of these is special syntax.
- To get several shots, you can simply let the default run, or take control of the cuts with timing instructions.
- Cutting rhythm is directable in plain words: "every 2s cut to a new frame", or "in a rapid fire sequence, change the scene to a new location every half a second".
- When you want a specific number of beats, describe them rather than counting shots. The model blocks the coverage from the described action.
- Camera vocabulary the owner lists: "static", "locked off" and "fixed" for a still frame; "push in", "punch in" and "dolly zoom" for moves; "natural smartphone zoom", "film camera" and "webcam style" for the kind of camera.
- Lock the camera to a subject when it must stay framed through a move: "The camera stays locked to the runner for the whole take."

</rules>

<example use_case="rapid-fire-sequence">

```text
A rapid fire sequence showing one unusual object per letter of the alphabet, each sitting alone on a plain table, roughly nine frames per item. Each object gets a lower third that looks like black marker written on a slip of paper in the bottom left, showing only its letter. One object and one lower third on screen at a time. The last frame is a slip of paper reading "THE END". Calm smooth music throughout.
```

*Why: takes control of the default cutting instead of suppressing it, fixes the pacing, the on-screen label style, and the one-at-a-time constraint, and pins a specific final frame*

</example>

## Timing and event synchronization

Two different clocks are available. Use wall-clock timing to choreograph, and event triggers to react to something already in the footage.

<rules id="timing">

- Wall-clock timing takes natural language and needs no precise syntax: "after 3 seconds, a woman enters the scene", "at 5s the chorus starts in the background audio".
- A bracketed timecode list is also accepted for laying out a whole shot as a timeline.
- In an extension, the clock restarts: 0s is the first moment of the new part, not of the original clip.
- Event triggers bind a change to a moment in the supplied video rather than to a time: "When the person touches the mirror, ...", "When the hand opens, ...". This is the most precise way to place an effect in footage you did not shoot to a click track.
- Audio can be the trigger or the target. Sounds can be synchronized to an action, and visual events can be synchronized to the music.
- One trigger per prompt. Chaining several conditional events in a single instruction is where this technique starts to fail.

</rules>

<template id="timecode">

```text
[0-3s] {what happens first}
[3-6s] {what changes}
[6-10s] {where it resolves}
```

</template>

<example use_case="timecode-timeline">

```text
[0-5s] A person is walking down a quiet street
[5-10s] They stop and turn around toward a glowing doorway
[10-15s] They start running into the portal
```

*Why: the owner's timecode example. Three beats sit on an explicit clock, each a single action, so the shot has a beginning, a turn and a payoff without any shot counting*

</example>

<example use_case="event-triggered-edit">

```text
When the person touches the mirror, make the mirror ripple beautifully like liquid, and the person's arm turns into reflective mirror material. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: the change is bound to an action already in the source footage rather than to a timestamp, so the effect lands on the frame where it belongs no matter how the take was shot*

</example>

<example use_case="audio-synchronized-effect">

```text
Add harp sounds synchronized to when I touch each fern leaf. Change the leaves to semi-translucent bioluminescent plant life with fireflies drifting around them that react in time with the sounds, subtle bokeh depth of field, light reflecting onto the walls of the room. Keep the room structure the same.
```

*Why: sound is generated on a visual cue and the visual reaction is then locked to that sound, using the same trigger in both directions, with the room pinned so only the named elements change*

</example>

## Audio

<rules id="audio">

- A soundtrack is generated whether or not you ask, so describe the one you want when it matters. Music especially will otherwise be the model's choice.
- Say what kind of music and where it sits: "include calm background music", "a high energy techno beat", "a low tinny radio broadcast in the background, playing a song".
- Describe the acoustic space as well as the sources: "both voices low and close", "her voice close and slightly boxy off the tile".
- Ask for a voiceover explicitly when you want narration. It is not implied by the presence of a subject.
- Suppress what you do not want with plain negatives: "No dialogue", "No extra sound effects", "No music, just realistic real world sound", "no voice".
- Watch the tail of the clip. "No voice cuts at the end" is a documented fix for narration that runs out of room.

</rules>

<example use_case="narrated-explainer">

```text
A claymation explainer of protein folding, everything made out of clay, stop motion, with a compelling voiceover explaining each stage accurately. No hands in frame. No music, just the narration and subtle stop-motion foley. Do not add text.
```

*Why: names the medium and the technique so the model supplies their conventions, calls for the voiceover explicitly rather than assuming it, and uses three plain negatives to strip the things this format usually attracts*

</example>

## Dialogue and voices

<rules id="dialogue">

- Name the speaker by something visible, then a colon, then the line: `The man in the blue sweater replies: "Did your father go out on the boat too?"`.
- Direct the delivery inline, before the colon: "says, quietly:", "answers without lifting his eyes from the timetable:".
- Keep lines short enough to fit the clip around the action. A clip carrying a camera move has less room for words than a static one.
- Speech can be given as a topic instead of a script: "looks directly at the camera and talks about what the final chapter will end with".
- Google's Omni examples put the spoken line in quotes. Google's shared Omni and Veo best-practice page drops the quotes after the colon instead, so the words are not rendered as on-screen text. If spoken lines start appearing as captions, drop the quotes and add "No subtitles".
- For one voice across several clips, name the character and give the voice a fixed description ("in a voice that is crisp and clear, with a thoughtful, analytical tone and a standard American accent"), then repeat the whole character and voice description verbatim in every prompt.
- Describe voices in words. Audio references are not part of the public API; the owner shows sampled voices only on gated and app surfaces.
- To keep a scene silent, say so: "No dialogue" or "no voice".

</rules>

<example use_case="two-speaker-flagship">

```text
A handoff scene from a Cold War spy picture, shot on a night express. One continuous shot, no cuts. It opens on a long lens down the corridor of a sleeper car, a woman in a gray traveling coat walking toward the camera with a leather document case, compartment doors sliding past her on both sides. She stops beside a man in a homburg reading a timetable, sets the case down on the floor without looking at him, and says, quietly: "The Vienna train doesn't stop tonight." He answers without lifting his eyes from the timetable: "Then neither do we." The camera pushes in past her shoulder as she keeps walking out of frame. Tungsten practicals flickering with the track, heavy 35mm grain, deep green shadow the length of the corridor. Rails under the floor, glass rattling in the compartment doors, both voices low and close, no music. No titles or subtitles.
```

*Why: fal's prompt and the flagship here. It names the genre, holds one take, blocks the action beat by beat, gives each speaker a visible identity and an inline delivery, keeps both lines short enough for the move, then closes with the voices' acoustic space and the negatives that stop the dialogue turning into captions*

</example>

## Text in video

<rules id="text">

- Quote the words you want rendered. The model places and animates text legibly, including per-word timing and animation.
- Name incidental background text too. Signs, storefronts, and number plates will otherwise be invented; specifying them is how you control them.
- Text can be bound to timing and to actions, which is how you get word-by-word reveals and lower thirds that change on cue.
- Suppress unwanted lettering with a plain "Do not add text." or "No on-screen text."

</rules>

<example use_case="word-by-word-text">

```text
One word on the screen at a time: "did, you, know, that, Omni, can, do, awesome, text?" Each word appears for one second in a different animated style, paced to a rhythm. Sizzle reel energy, upbeat music. No dialogue.
```

*Why: the full string is quoted, the per-word timing and the style variation are stated as rules rather than described per word, and the negative stops the words being spoken as well as shown*

</example>

<example use_case="background-text">

```text
There is a street sign that says: "This is an AI generation by Omni", there is a storefront that says: "All you need AI", there's a car with the number plate: "OMNI1.2"
```

*Why: the owner's example for incidental text. Every surface that would otherwise carry invented lettering is named with its exact words*

</example>

## References and roles

Images and short video clips can be supplied as references for a subject, a place, a style or a motion. Say in plain words what each one is for.

<rules id="references">

- Do not write the angle-bracket control tags (`<FIRST_FRAME>`, `<LAST_FRAME>`, `<IMAGE_REF_0>`, `<VIDEO_REF_0>`). The owner has deprecated them and says they are unsupported in future models. Some provider guides written for 1.1 still use them; translate them into plain words.
- Name each input by what it shows: "the woman in the reference image", "the corridor image", "the rooftop courtyard image", "the camera move in the reference video".
- One role per input. Give each reference exactly one job: identity, location, style, camera move, or performance.
- Name what moves, pin what stays. Say which traits carry over from which reference: "Her face, her hair and the camel overcoat carry over from the reference image unchanged, and the sconces and the shadow pooling carry over from the corridor."
- When the subjects are obvious from the images, a plain description is enough; the owner's cat-and-yarn example never mentions the images. Spell out roles once several inputs could fill the same slot.
- Several views of one character can be supplied to hold a likeness and an outfit. Describe them as one character.
- A reference video is a visual specification, for likeness, camera or performance; any audio in it is ignored. Say exactly what to take from it ("Match the camera to the dolly move in the reference video, same speed and same height off the floor"), and trim it to one clean beat with a start and a finish.
- Reference videos are for new generations only. Edits and extensions take reference images, not reference clips.
- The owner's docs say reasoning across several videos is unsupported and can degrade the result, although its own launch demo maps three dance clips onto three characters. Tie each clip to one character or one job, and expect less reliability with every clip you add.
- When an input is a guide rather than content, say so and say what must not appear: "using the drawing only as a guide for movement, do not show the drawing in the final video".
- A storyboard image works as an input. Say which way to read it and that the story should be followed in order.
- Google's showcase and prompt-guide pages also show lowercase `<image>`, `<video>`, `<object>` and `<audio>` placeholders. Those are app-surface forms; through an API, use plain words.

</rules>

<example use_case="multi-source-reference">

```text
The woman in the reference portrait walks the length of the stone corridor shown in the corridor image, carrying a tan leather weekender bag at her side. Match the camera to the dolly move in the reference video, same speed and same height off the floor. Her face, her hair and the camel overcoat carry over from the reference portrait unchanged, and the sconces and the shadow pooling carry over from the corridor. One continuous shot, no cuts. Heels on stone, the bag handle creaking, a distant air handler, no music. No text of any kind in frame.
```

*Why: fal's three-input prompt with its role tags replaced by plain names, as the owner now requires. Each input gets one job (identity, location, camera move), the carried traits are listed per source, and the continuity, sound and text constraints close it out*

</example>

<example use_case="cross-modal-transfer">

```text
Apply the pose and motion from the input video to the knight in the character image, and apply the style of the watercolor reference to the new video. Keep the knight's proportions and armor as they are in the character image.
```

*Why: built from the owner's showcase pattern ("Apply the pose and motion from input video to provided character from this image. Apply style from image reference to the new video"). One role per input across three sources, each named by its content, with the invariants named so the borrowed motion does not drag the character's design with it*

</example>

<example use_case="storyboard-input">

```text
Show me in this story. Follow the story exactly in order starting top left. Entire story in 10 seconds. Cinematic
```

*Why: the owner's storyboard prompt, given a photo of the user and a panel storyboard. It states the reading order and that the panels are followed in sequence; the length in the prompt holds only when the request sets none*

</example>

## By mode

### Text to video

<example use_case="text-to-video">

```text
A marble rolling fast on a chain reaction style track, continuous smooth shot. Close following camera, shallow depth of field, warm workshop lighting. Sound design: the marble ticking over wooden rails, springs releasing, a distant clock. No music.
```

*Why: a short concrete subject and a continuity instruction do most of the work, and the sound-design tail gives the audio track something specific to build from*

</example>

### Image to video

<rules id="i2v">

- Say what role the image plays. The model decides for itself otherwise, and a supplied drawing may end up rendered in frame rather than animated.
- Prompt for motion: camera movement, subject motion, and environmental effects. The image already supplies the subject, the scene and the style, and re-describing them confuses the model.
- Refer to the subject in general terms ("the subject", "the woman", "she") rather than describing them again.
- Name the parts of the still that must not change when they matter: "Nothing about the mirror, the shelf or either light source should change from the still."
- Use a high-resolution source; vague prompts on a soft image produce the weakest results in this mode.

</rules>

<example use_case="image-to-video">

```text
Turn this into realistic footage, using the drawing only as a guide for movement, do not show the drawing in the final video. The fish breaks the surface, water sheeting off its fins, spray catching the light before it falls back. Slow motion, shallow depth of field. Sound design: the splash and a low underwater rumble.
```

*Why: states the image's role and forbids the obvious failure (rendering the drawing itself), then supplies the motion, the physical detail, and the audio that the still cannot carry*

</example>

### First and last frame

<rules id="first-last">

- Describe what the opening frame shows, the camera move or transition path that joins the two, and what the final frame reveals. This mode re-describes the endpoints, unlike single-image animation.
- Name the move that carries you from one to the other: a whip pan, a zoom into a screen, an orbit around a product.
- For a loop, use the same image at both ends and say the clip returns to where it began.
- When both frames should share a set, make the last frame by editing the first rather than generating two separate images. A near-miss pair forces the model to reconcile the gap somewhere in the clip, and it picks where.
- Close with a continuity instruction ("One continuous shot, no jump cuts."), or the model may cut between the two frames.
- This mode runs shorter clips than single-image animation; a host may refuse a long duration here.

</rules>

<example use_case="first-last-transition">

```text
A close-up low-angle shot of a stylish drummer in a beige suit playing a red drum kit in a grand hall transitions as the camera whip-pans to the side, revealing an older saxophonist playing alongside a ballet dancer spinning in a white outfit under soft purple stage lights. One continuous shot, no jump cuts.
```

*Why: the owner's example. The first frame is described, the whip pan names the path between, the final frame's content is stated as what the pan reveals, and the closing line stops a hard cut from replacing the move*

</example>

<example use_case="first-last-loop">

```text
The camera zooms into the TV screen, where we see the same woman and the same scene from the beginning. Seamless video. One continuous shot, no jump cuts.
```

*Why: the owner's loop, with the same image given as both frames. The zoom is the path, and the screen showing the opening scene closes the loop back onto the first frame*

</example>

### Reference to video

<example use_case="reference-to-video">

```text
When the hand opens, reveal a physical photorealistic flying machine based on this sketch, floating above the hand, propeller spinning. No music, just realistic sound.
```

*Why: the owner's showcase prompt. It combines an event trigger with a sketch supplied as the design source, named in plain words, and strips the score so the effect reads as physical*

</example>

## Extending a video

An extension continues a clip from its end. It works on clips the model made and on uploaded clips, and each extension is its own turn.

<rules id="extend">

- Write what happens next, not what already happened: "Extend this video", "The scene continues", or "Continue the video." followed by the next beat.
- Say whether the scene carries on or cuts to a new scene, perhaps with the same characters: "Show the same characters in the next scene".
- Describe the audio of the new part, especially when it changes: "The music continues into the chorus".
- The model only looks at the last stretch of the clip. If a character or element appeared only earlier, mention it again or pass it as a reference image.
- A reference image can bring a new character in: "Extend this video: have the character shown in the reference image enter the scene and wave."
- Timestamps count from the start of the extension, not of the original clip.
- The last few frames of the input may be re-rendered to smooth the join; do not rely on them staying pixel-identical.
- Camera moves, music and new dialogue can all be carried in the extension text, one turn at a time.

</rules>

<example use_case="extension-camera">

```text
Continue the video. Execute a cinematic optical dolly-zoom shot. The camera dollies forward while simultaneously zooming out, keeping the character's frozen shocked face locked at the exact same size. The long corridor of stone pillars in the background dramatically stretches and deepens with intense optical perspective distortion. Clean architecture, continuous unbroken shot.
```

*Why: the owner's extension prompt. It opens with the continuation cue, describes only the new camera move and what it does to the frame, and closes with the continuity instruction so the extension does not cut away*

</example>

<example use_case="extension-dialogue">

```text
The camera pulls back in one continuous movement as he continues his story: "He used to say that this harbour had a soul. And that the boats were a part of us." The music swells.
```

*Why: the second turn of the owner's sailor chain. The speaker is already established by the earlier clip, so the extension carries only the camera move, the next line and the change in the music*

</example>

## Conversational editing

Editing is stateful across turns. Each instruction builds on the last while the model preserves what you did not mention, so make one change per turn.

<rules id="edit">

- Lead with an imperative verb. It can name the change (Add, Replace, Change, Make) or the constraint (Keep), but the opening clause must instruct rather than describe. A declarative setup such as "Image 1 provides the foundation, Image 2 provides the face" states the plan instead of issuing it and pushes the real instruction back by a sentence or two.
- Keep it short. A single sentence naming one change outperforms a paragraph describing the whole result.
- Add "Keep everything else the same" whenever you are changing one aspect. It is the standard invariant phrase in this scheme. When specific things were hard to get right, list them instead: one sentence of change, one sentence of what to keep.
- When chaining edits, say what to keep "from the previous pass".
- Name the outcome, not the procedure. "Make the phone invisible" beats describing removal and background reconstruction.
- Make one change per turn and iterate. Stacking edits into one instruction is what causes drift.
- Camera, style, lighting, weather, background, products and on-screen text are all editable in plain words on an existing clip. The owner lists switching the aspect ratio among 1.2's conversational edits.
- Reference images can be added to an edit to show what something should become.

</rules>

<example use_case="simple-edit">

```text
Add a cat that jumps onto his lap, he begins to pet it. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: the short form of an edit the owner shows failing when written out in full; naming the outcome and pinning the rest is what keeps the change scoped*

</example>

<example use_case="removal-edit">

```text
Make the phone invisible. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: states the desired end state in three words rather than describing removal and background fill, which is the phrasing that produces clean removals here*

</example>

<example use_case="camera-edit">

```text
Change the camera angle, a close-up on his shoes, quickly tilting up to medium shot, then widening.
```

*Why: the owner's camera edit. Framing and movement are editable properties of an existing clip, and the new move is given as a short sequence rather than a description of the result*

</example>

<example use_case="preserve-list-edit">

```text
Put the bag on a pale limestone block and warm the background to a soft sand tone. Keep the bag itself, the key light, the shadow direction and the camera move exactly as they are.
```

*Why: fal's product edit. One sentence of change and one of preservation, with the preserve list naming the four things that were expensive to get right instead of a blanket phrase*

</example>

<example use_case="style-transfer">

```text
Make this video anime. Keep everything else the same.
```

*Why: a pure style transfer from the owner's simple-edit list with the standard invariant phrase. The look changes and the action, framing and timing stay*

</example>

<example use_case="style-transfer-plus-edit">

```text
Regrade the corridor to faded Eastmancolor with magenta-shifted highlights and visible gate weave, and change the man's homburg to a flat cap. Keep both faces, the dialogue, the rest of the wardrobe, and the camera move.
```

*Why: fal's edit, pairing a look change with one wardrobe swap and naming everything that must survive both. It carries two changes in one turn; if either drifts, split them across two turns*

</example>

## Negative prompts and exclusions

<rules id="negatives">

- There is no negative field, and none is needed. Write plain negatives into the prompt: "No dialogue", "No embellishments", "No extra sound effects", "Don't add text", "No titles or subtitles", "no wires", "no hands".
- This is the opposite of the Gemini Image rule. Do not rewrite these into positive descriptions.
- Put them at the end, in short blunt clauses, so they read as constraints rather than scene content.
- Pair a negative with the positive it protects when the distinction matters: "No music, just realistic real world sound".

</rules>

## Requests the model will not fulfil

<rules id="unsupported">

- Do not ask an extension to add footage before or in the middle of a clip. Extension only appends to the end.
- Do not ask to extend an uploaded clip in which someone is talking with more dialogue. Silent extensions of uploaded clips work, and extending the model's own clips can add speech.
- Do not supply a reference clip to an edit or an extension. Reference clips are for new generations; use reference images there.
- Do not ask for voice editing or for an edit that changes what someone says. The owner restricts both.
- Do not plan on an audio input or an audio reference through the API; describe the sound in words.
- Do not expect a system-instruction layer or a separate negative field to shape behavior. Everything goes in the prompt.
- Regional restrictions apply to editing and extending uploaded video and to uploaded images of certain people; these vary by host and are out of scope here.

</rules>

## Pitfalls and anti-patterns

<rules id="avoid">

- Unwanted cuts: the default is a multi-shot narrative. Ask for a single continuous shot when you want one take, including in first-and-last-frame mode.
- Over-describing an edit: long editing instructions cause unintended changes. Name one outcome and add "Keep everything else the same".
- Rewriting negatives as positives: a habit carried from the Gemini Image scheme. Plain negatives are the documented advice here.
- Writing control tags: `<FIRST_FRAME>`, `<IMAGE_REF_0>` and their kin are deprecated. Describe each input's role in words.
- Unassigned references: with several inputs, leaving roles to inference blends them. Name each by what it shows and give it one job.
- Letting a guide image render: state when an input is only a movement or style guide and forbid it appearing in frame.
- Re-describing the still in image-to-video: the image supplies the look; spend the prompt on motion.
- Spoken lines turning into captions: drop the quotes after the colon and add "No subtitles".
- Forgetting early elements in an extension: the model reads only the end of the clip, so restate or re-supply anything from earlier.
- Clocking an extension from the original clip: timestamps restart at the start of the extension.
- Forcing the mode: try plain prompting before a host's mode switch, which adds constraints.
- Silent audio assumptions: a soundtrack is generated regardless. Describe it or suppress it.
- Unspecified background text: signs and labels get invented. Name them if they matter.
- Chained event triggers: one "when X happens" per prompt. Several conditions in one instruction is where this breaks down.
- Non-English prompts: only English is evaluated, so results in other languages are unpredictable.
- Vague motion in image-to-video: "make it move" wastes the mode. Describe camera, subject, and environment motion separately.

</rules>
